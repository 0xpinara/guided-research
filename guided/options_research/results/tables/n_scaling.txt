Note. Moving from bare significance to 80% power multiplies the required
effect by (3+0.84)/3 = 1.28. That costs a factor 1.64 in N under 1/sqrt(N)
but a factor 2.36 under the measured N^-0.289 law, because a flatter exponent
means each unit of extra resolution buys far fewer names. Reliable detection
is therefore substantially more expensive than the conventional rule implies.

Reading. A pure-noise placebo (--placebo noise) recovers an exponent of about
-0.5 with no variance floor, so the subsampling machinery is sound. On the
real predictions the exponent is materially flatter than -0.5 and the
SE^2 = a + b/N fit carries a positive intercept. The reason is that the HAC SE
of the MEAN per-window IC combines two terms: cross-sectional sampling noise,
which does shrink with N, and genuine time-variation in the cross-sectional IC,
which does not. The second term puts a floor under the standard error that no
universe size removes at a fixed number of evaluation windows. The plain
1/sqrt(N) calculator is therefore OPTIMISTIC: it understates the cross-section
needed, and for effects below 3*sqrt(a) it reports a finite N where the honest
answer is that more names alone will not do it -- more calendar time, or a
panel-level estimator with a smaller floor, is required.
